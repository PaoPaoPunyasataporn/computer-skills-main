AI LITERACY CURRICULUM
Computer Skills Platform - AI Course
Five modules, twelve lessons


ABOUT THIS DOCUMENT

This document lists the modules and lessons that make up the AI literacy course,
describes what each lesson teaches, and gives the source for every factual claim
or teaching framework the lesson content is based on. Citation numbers refer to
the reference list at the end of this document. Lessons that do not carry a
citation are original design work (game mechanics, scoring, scenario writing)
and are not based on an external source.

Each lesson runs through the same five parts: a short hook framing a real
situation, a brief concept explanation, a hands-on activity with feedback after
each step, a short reflection question, and a completion score. Lesson files
are self-contained HTML pages stored in public/games/ and are listed by file
name below.


MODULE 1 - FOUNDATIONS

Lesson: What AI Is and How It Works
File: ai0-ai-foundations.html

Teaches that an AI language model produces text by predicting one word (or
word-fragment) at a time based on patterns learned from training data, rather
than by reasoning the way a person does. Also teaches that a model can state a
wrong answer with the same apparent confidence as a correct one, because the
model has no built-in way to signal uncertainty. The hands-on activity has
students sort true and false statements about AI, then match AI-related terms
to their definitions.
Sources: 1, 4, 5

Lesson: AI Trainer
File: ai6-model-trainer.html

Teaches the basic loop behind supervised machine learning: label a set of
examples, train a model on those labels, then test the model on new examples
it has not seen. Also teaches that a model trained on a narrow or unbalanced
set of examples will make mistakes or unfair predictions when it meets
something outside that set. The hands-on activity has students label images,
flag mislabeled data, retrain a model, and answer questions about why a
model failed and which training set would be fairest.
Sources: 1, 3, 4, 6

Lesson: Bias Detective
File: ai7-bias-detective.html

Teaches how an unbalanced training set produces a biased model, using four
scenarios: a fruit-sorting camera, a face-recognition door, a voice assistant,
and a photo app that misjudges lighting on darker skin tones. In each case
students inspect a data table, decide why the system is failing, choose or
build a fix, and see the model retrained and retested.
Sources: 1, 3


MODULE 2 - WORKING WITH AI

Lesson: Chat Interface Explorer
File: ai1-ui-inspector.html

Teaches the parts of a typical AI chat interface, such as the send button, the
stop-generating button, model switching, and chat history, along with the
concept of a token as the basic unit an AI model reads and writes text in. The
hands-on activity is a guided tour where students perform each action in a
mock chat interface, followed by an exercise ordering tokens correctly and a
short quiz identifying interface elements.
No external source cited; content is based on common conventions shared by
mainstream AI chat products.

Lesson: Prompt Fixer
File: ai2-prompt-fixer.html

Teaches that a clear instruction to an AI system generally needs three parts:
who the AI should act as, what it should do, and what form the answer should
take. Students first pick the better of several sample prompts, then rewrite
weak prompts themselves and see them checked against these three parts.
Sources: 7, 8

Lesson: Discernment
File: ai10-discernment.html

Teaches students to check an AI's answer before trusting it, rather than
trusting it because it sounds confident. The hands-on activity presents
sixteen AI answers, some correct, some wrong, and some biased; students find
the specific part of the answer that gives it away, then classify the answer
as trustworthy, needing a check, or clearly wrong. One round uses a fabricated
sprint time to illustrate a false but plausible-sounding claim. The lesson
name follows the general use of the word "discernment" in AI literacy
teaching, including Anthropic's AI Fluency course, which names critical
evaluation of AI output as one of four core competencies. No text, scenario,
or exercise from that course was copied; the lesson content here is original.
Sources: 2, 5, 11, 12

Lesson: Diligence
File: ai11-diligence.html

Teaches when it is acceptable to use AI for a task, when it is acceptable but
must be disclosed, and when it is not acceptable, based on common academic
integrity guidance. The hands-on activity sorts sixteen scenarios into these
three categories and asks students to write a short disclosure sentence for
scenarios that require one. The lesson name follows the same general use of
the word "diligence" as Anthropic's AI Fluency course, which names
responsible creation, transparency, and ownership of AI-assisted work as one
of four core competencies. No text, scenario, or exercise from that course
was copied; the lesson content here is original.
Sources: 2, 9, 10, 12


MODULE 3 - SHAPING AI BEHAVIOR

Lesson: Gem Architect
File: ai4-gem-architect.html

Teaches how to give an AI assistant a persona and a set of rules, including
the idea that a rule works better when it pairs something the assistant should
not do with something it should do instead. Students answer warm-up questions
about rule design, then build a set of rules for an AI tutor and watch it
tested against simulated student requests.
Sources: 7, 8

Lesson: Sandbox Master
File: ai5-sandbox-master.html

Teaches that settings such as a creativity level and an assigned role change
the tone and content of an AI's response. Students experiment freely in a
simulated chat with a creativity slider and a role selector, then answer
prediction questions about how a given setting would change the output.
No external source cited; content demonstrates a general property of
generative AI systems rather than a specific claim requiring a citation.


MODULE 4 - AI IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Lesson: Learn With AI
File: ai3-learn-with-ai.html

Teaches how to use an AI chat assistant as a study aid across school subjects.
Students work through several topics, each combining a simulated chat
exchange with practice questions in different formats, including multiple
choice, fill in the blank, matching, and ordering steps.
No external source cited; topic content (fractions, multiplication, vocabulary)
is standard school curriculum material.

Lesson: AI in the World
File: ai8-ai-in-the-world.html

Teaches that AI is present in many everyday applications, not only chatbots,
and that some uses of AI are safe while others carry risk. Students identify
which of several systems is not an AI example, match AI systems to the kind
of data likely used to train them, and sort everyday scenarios into safe and
risky categories.
Sources: 1, 2


MODULE 5 - CAPSTONE

Lesson: AI Quest
File: ai9-ai-quest.html

A closing project that asks students to design an AI system end to end: state
a goal, choose training data, review test results, check whether the system
generalizes to new cases, check the system for bias, decide who should be
told the system is in use, and launch it responsibly. This lesson combines
the skills taught in the earlier eleven lessons rather than introducing new
external material.
Sources: 2, 3

The lesson names "Discernment" and "Diligence" and the general idea of
teaching critical evaluation of AI output and responsible disclosure of AI
use are shared with Anthropic's AI Fluency course, cited above. These are
common English words used broadly across AI literacy education, not names or
text owned by Anthropic. No paragraph, scenario, exercise, image, or other
material was copied, paraphrased from, or adapted out of the Anthropic
course, the Experience AI curriculum, Elements of AI, or any other source
listed here. All lesson text, scenarios, and game mechanics in this course
were written independently for this platform. Every source above was checked
directly on the publisher's own site (openai.com, anthropic.com,
unesco.org, raspberrypi.org, elementsofai.com, teachablemachine.withgoogle.com,
ai4k12.org, cmu.edu, vanderbilt.edu, worldathletics.org, arxiv.org) rather
than through a secondary or unofficial mirror.
